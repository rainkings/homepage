Outburst systems are young stellar objects that experience a sudden and substantial increase in luminosity (Fischer+ 2023). During such outbursts, their circumstellar disks undergo intense heating, driving the snowlines outward. As the system fades, the disk cools and the snowlines retreat. Owing to their expanded snowline locations, which are spatially resolvable with ALMA, outburst systems represent ideal sites to study disk snowline.

**V883 Ori** is an FU-Orionis-type outburst system whose ALMA Band 6 and 7 images reveal a distinct shoulder at 50–70 au in the dust continuum intensity profile (Cieza+ 2016, Houge+ 2024). This feature was previously attributed to solid pile-up from pebble disintegration at the water snowline. However, recent multi-band observations show continuity in the spectral index across the expected snowline region, disfavoring abrupt changes in grain properties. Moreover, extended water emission is detected beyond 80 au, indicating a snowline located farther out (Tobin+ 2023).

While earlier interpretations relied on static disk models, the snowline in V883 Ori is in fact retreating as the system fades, making time-dependent modeling essential. We therefore construct a 2D disk model that self-consistently solves the cooling and subsequent vapor recondensation during the post-outburst dimming phase.

The model naturally reproduces both the observed intensity shoulder and the extended water vapor as relics of a retreating snowline:
(1) at the moving condensation front, the recondensation of vapor enhances the local surface density, producing the observed intensity shoulder (panel 2); and
(2) While in the midplane, vapor recondenses instanteneously with the retreat of snowline, in the disk atmosphere, the recondensation timescale in the disk atmosphere becomes long ($10^2$-$10^3$ yrs) due to the low pebble density there. The resulting lingering water plumes (pink in panel 3) naturally explain the extended water emission.
As V883 Ori continues to fade, we predict that the intensity shoulder will migrate inward by an observationally significant ∼10 au over the next ∼25 years.
